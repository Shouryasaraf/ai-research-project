# Uncertainty-Gated Predictive Warm Migration for Carbon- and Attack-Risk-Aware Stateful Virtual Firewall Placement

**Evidence-aligned working manuscript; not submission-ready.** Author information, venue, final protocol and independent confirmatory evaluation are intentionally unset. This draft describes a prototype and exploratory findings, not established novelty or superiority. Results through 2026-10-13 are included; immutable generated reports remain the source for exact trial records.

## Abstract

Stateful virtual-firewall orchestration must balance workload accommodation against migration disruption and security constraints. We implement an auditable controller combining traffic-forecast intervals, constrained placement, a contextual benefit threshold and guarded state/route transitions. Linux namespace testbeds on a development WSL2 host and an owner-attested second native-Linux device exercise persistent application flows through kernel-enforced firewall paths; external packet captures supply **rate schedules**, not replayed attack or industrial packets. Across three preselected Cyber4OT PLC-port normal traces, the fixed uncertainty gate prevented all 15 proposed-policy route commits while reactive control committed in every paired trial, lowering proposed-policy mean goodput and raising trial-wide p95 latency. A separately named liveness escape recovered commits in a development workload but retained high pre-transition latency and did not beat reactive mean goodput. A protocol-frozen second Zero-SWARM normal file showed tiny mixed differences because the escape was never activated. On the second device, 18 captured synthetic trials reproduced the lab pipeline and another negative full-versus-reactive goodput comparison even though both policies committed once per trial. Captured trials had no application failures but packet-instance deficits and repeated payload sequences prevent zero-loss or full TCP-state claims. Carbon and security risk remain modeled. The evidence supports bounded characterization of policy failure modes, not superiority, general migration correctness or established novelty.

## 1. Problem and contribution boundary

The system has seven locked functions: dynamic instance scaling, uncertainty/security-aware placement, load distribution, stateful warm transition with flow affinity, uncertainty-gated migration, adaptive benefit threshold and invariant-driven fallback. The research question is whether their particular coordination improves service and migration trade-offs under calibrated uncertainty. Merely combining components does not establish novelty.

The present artifact contributes an executable experimental integration, auditable policy comparisons, a documented uncertainty-gate liveness counterexample, and explicit evidence boundaries. It does not claim that state movement, virtual-firewall elasticity, uncertainty-aware placement or cost-aware migration are new individually. The experimental guard is a post-result development response, not part of the original submitted controller's confirmatory result.

## 2. Related work

[Deng et al.](https://cse.buffalo.edu/~hongxinh/papers/NDSS2017.pdf) directly study safe virtual-firewall elasticity, including rule/state movement, flow updates, semantic consistency, buffer cost and scaling. This is a stronger architectural comparator for functions 1, 3, 4 and 7 than a generic VNF paper; our two-node fixed-rule lab does not reproduce its safety guarantees. [OpenNF](https://wisr.cs.wisc.edu/papers/opennf.pdf) coordinates network-function state with forwarding updates and addresses races during state movement. Its guarantees are not implied by our coarse conntrack copying. [Forti et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/1906.00197) study probabilistic infrastructure and security-aware VNF placement. [Ibrahimpasic et al.](https://arxiv.org/abs/2101.09343) balance stateful migration cost against outage loss. [ESSO](https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~mfbari/files/j8.pdf) studies energy-aware service-chain relocation. Accordingly, our candidate distinction is the specific uncertainty-gated decision sequence and its measured trade-offs, not a novel generic VFW controller. The full-text/citation-chain review remains incomplete; see `direct_vfw_comparator.md` and the machine-readable prior-art matrix.

The later primary-source screen adds [Patowary et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2404.07701v1), who formalize a stateful flow-migration correctness property; [Shang et al.](https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9248649/), who account for prediction errors and migration costs in online SFC placement; and [Peretz et al.](https://deepness-lab.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/201585_1.pdf), who use security-motivated VNF state movement. [Mahyoub et al.](https://arxiv.org/html/2607.00471v1) jointly address security risk and migration in a satellite SFC setting. These overlaps narrow the proposed claim and demand direct method comparisons before any novelty assertion.

[Yuan et al.](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/10084175/1/nfvreplication_MR-R3.pdf) jointly consider active/standby stateful VNF placement, routing, state-transfer paths and failure recovery; [Xing et al.](https://www.cs.rice.edu/~eugeneng/papers/SPIN20.pdf) study authenticated data-plane state migration. Our modeled risk score and firewall-policy hash comparison are not evidence of attack reduction or authenticated state transport.

## 3. Method

Candidates first pass hard policy, health, capacity and security restrictions. Only eligible candidates are ranked using configured resource, latency, carbon, risk and uncertainty-headroom weights. Soft-score ablations retain hard safety constraints.

The controller uses forecast uncertainty to decide whether to wait, prepare or synchronize a warm target. A commit requires supported state validation and a positive margin between normalized expected benefit and the contextual threshold. Benefits and penalties are logged separately. The current live experiment uses independently supplied modeled carbon/risk benefit credits, not a fitted causal emissions or attack-loss model.

The source-pinning proxy and transparent route lab are distinct mechanisms. The latter copies supported conntrack fields and changes both traffic directions. A durable journal records intent before each route change and verifies readback. On uncertain restart, containment precedes any restoration; stale source state is never assumed sufficient. This trades availability for conservative behavior and is not an atomic distributed commit.

## 4. Experimental setup

See `paper_methods_draft.md` for implementation details and `live_experiment_protocol.md` for the initial frozen development design. All live experiments use one Linux/WSL host, six namespaces, default-deny nftables and tc-shaped source/target links. Persistent TCP echo requests measure completed-payload goodput and request round-trip delay. Completed interval observations, not future scheduled demand, drive forecasts.

Nine original policy variants share the same interface; the later liveness guard has a separate experimental name. Seed-paired schedules and shuffled execution order reduce avoidable comparison differences. The chronological external-trace split is 60% training, 20% calibration and 20% held-out test; rate scaling and forecast parameters use earlier segments only. Each trace-derived live pair reuses the first 30 held-out one-second rate bins as a generated TCP echo workload, so five seeds are execution repetitions, **not five independent traffic traces**. A separately frozen synthetic step/bursts/flash check ran on an owner-attested second native-Linux device using the same namespace architecture. Separate experiments examine modeled parameter sensitivity, real-process interruption and additional workload shapes. These answer different questions and must not be pooled as independent replications of one outcome.

## 5. Exploratory results

The original pilot numerical tables are generated from raw records in `results/live-paired-20261008-pilot/results.md` and `results/live-mawi-20261008-pilot/results.md`; the manuscript paraphrases them and points to the generated records for exact values. The synthetic pilot favors the proposed method over static placement but favors reactive control over the proposed method. Successful warm-path requests and failures in the cold baseline illustrate the importance of state handling in the tested conditions, not a general losslessness guarantee.

In the historical rate-derived segment, different commit decisions coexist with similar achieved goodput. The segment therefore does not demonstrate improved throughput from prediction. Repetitions reuse the same segment and cannot establish cross-workload generalization. The modeled sensitivity grid changes contextual decisions but does not measure emissions, attack reduction or temporal migration frequency.

A second, 2005-01-01 sample from the same MAWI samplepoint passed the publisher's packet and byte totals. Its 181-second held-out portion led the full policy to COMMIT on almost all independent ready-target opportunities, in contrast with all WAIT on the earlier 2000 sample. The evaluator resets state each second, so these are not realized repeated migrations or net-cost outcomes. A six-trial captured, scaled-payload lab pair on the second trace also favored reactive goodput slightly and observed reverse packet-instance deficits under both policies. The complete negative/mixed transfer result is documented in `results/mawi-transfer-20261010/results.md` and the validated live table in `results/live-mawi2005-captured-pair-20261010-v1/`. Neither time segment is an independent site or a modern vFW workload.

A finite-placement post-hoc audit makes the reset limitation explicit: under a single target that stays selected after a first possible move, 179 of the 180 fixed-EWMA full-policy COMMIT recommendations on the second segment address the already-selected target. This is an upper-bound logic check, not executed sequential migration or a cost/anti-thrashing estimate. A real sequential comparison must recompute forecasts, placement, benefit and safety after each transition.

The expanded modeled grid pairs candidate choices across 243 identical opportunities for all nine policies. Removing the carbon term changes 81 choices and removing the soft risk term changes 27 relative to the full controller, while the hard-prohibited node remains excluded. Candidate choices are recorded even for `WAIT` actions, so these counts neither represent deployments nor quantify real emissions or attacks. The generated table is in `results/sensitivity-20261010-placement-v3/`.

Restart probes establish blocked-route containment followed by source restoration when no active TCP state exists. The journal-integrated live smoke separately checks ordinary migration operation. Neither result establishes automatic recovery of established flows after controller or target failure.

The later active-recovery study extends this boundary: a supervised migration-process exit with surviving target state allowed original-flow failback after reverse synchronization of supported fields. Target-state deletion instead retained blocked routes and disrupted the flows. Verified endpoint captures expose packet-instance deficits even where application requests recovered. The all-attempt report in `results/recovery-20261009/` preserves failed development attempts and the corrected final batch. This is still not full-host recovery, full TCP-state equivalence or zero-loss migration.

Six later proxy lifecycle repetitions functionally connect flow-occupancy-derived modeled demand, scale-out, predictive prewarm, pinned-state validation, guarded target admission, drain, and scale-in. The generated `results/lifecycle-20261009-snapshotted/` report reads their raw records and verifies exact source snapshots for the final three. This proxy mechanism does not transfer conntrack and is not a comparative benchmark or measured carbon/risk experiment.

Separate method controls checked the endpoint packet observation: two ordinary traffic trials showed zero matched packet deficits, while two isolated deliberate-drop trials showed 12 missing forward packet instances each. The same application requests eventually completed, demonstrating that request-success counts alone would conceal these temporary wire-level drops. The generated `results/packet-validation-20261010/` report recomputes the paired captures and source fingerprint. These controls validate the measurement method in one topology; they are excluded from migration outcome comparisons.

A later captured three-seed full-versus-reactive development batch recorded a route commit in every trial and no request failures. Endpoint matching nevertheless found eight missing reverse packet instances in every trial. Reactive control again achieved higher mean goodput in the same synthetic schedule. The `results/live-captured-pair-20261010-v2/` tables are generated from complete raw batch records and verified captures. Neither the repeated deficit count nor this small paired difference establishes a general loss rate or policy ranking.

A post-hoc timing diagnostic finds the last successful same-flow response before each recorded transition and the first after it. These brackets include pacing and synchronization, so they are not outage estimates. Their policy ordering reverses between the synthetic step and second historical rate-derived batches; see `results/live-captured-pair-gap-20261010-v3/` and `results/live-mawi2005-captured-pair-gap-20261010-v2/`. Exact interruption remains unmeasured.

A protocol-frozen development transfer pilot used normal-traffic rate metadata from the 2024 [Cyber4OT](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13935639) simulated industrial-network archive, an independent publisher from MAWI. Five captured full/reactive pairs completed on the same WSL2 host. Full mean goodput was only 0.001408 Mbps higher, with a descriptive paired interval crossing zero, while both policies had packet-instance deficits in some runs and no application failures. This small source-transfer result is not a confirmatory advantage or direct replay of industrial packets; details are in `results/cyber4ot-transfer-20261010/results.md`.

A separate re-parse of those validated endpoint captures found no sender-side TCP RST in 12 migration trials, but repeated identical payload-sequence instances in all 12. These are bounded wire-visible observations, not proof that the two kernels held equivalent sequence/window state; see `tcp_wire_diagnostic.md` and its generated result tables.

An additional offline carbon-input check imports two aligned historical half-hourly regional **forecasts** from NESO's CC BY 4.0 Carbon Intensity API. When mapped to hypothetical candidates with all other inputs held modeled and fixed, full and carbon-unaware placement prefer different nodes in every slot. This demonstrates implementation sensitivity to a sourced carbon signal but does not estimate real power use or emissions. The provenance and decision boundary are in `results/neso-carbon-replay-20261010/`.

### 5.1. A reproducible fixed-gate liveness failure

Three PLC-port normal members of the [Cyber4OT archive](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.13935639) were selected by a committed protocol before conversion and before their live results were seen. Five captured, paired full/reactive seeds per member completed. The table summarizes means; descriptive paired intervals and all 30 trial records are in `results/cyber4ot-plc-port-20261010/`.

| Member | Full goodput (Mbps) | Reactive goodput (Mbps) | Full − reactive (Mbps) | Full/reactive physical commits | Full/reactive mean trial p95 RTT (ms) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| PLC 069 | 0.885515 | 0.890776 | -0.005261 | 0/5 | 51.612/0.401 |
| PLC 070 | 0.907403 | 0.952542 | -0.045139 | 0/5 | 57.923/0.407 |
| PLC 071 | 0.906875 | 0.946482 | -0.039607 | 0/5 | 54.971/0.419 |

All 435 full-policy decisions in these 15 trials were uncertainty WAITs. The fixed normalized split-conformal interval widths were 0.222692, 0.241013 and 0.229504, above the prespecified 0.20 commit gate. This is a concrete liveness failure, not an infrastructure error: all trials completed with valid endpoint captures and zero application request failures. The same time slice is reused within each member; the three members share one simulated OT corpus. Thus the result is stronger than a single-seed anecdote but weaker than independent production-workload confirmation. This negative result must remain visible in any submission.

### 5.2. Exploratory escape and distinct-file check

After observing member 070, we implemented a *separately named experimental* liveness guard that can synchronize and commit after three consecutive observed overload samples and three baseline uncertainty WAITs, while retaining safety, feasibility and benefit checks. Its 15-trial development comparison on member 070 yielded one commit per guard and reactive trial, versus none for original full. Mean goodput was 0.946494 Mbps (guard), 0.907485 (original full) and 0.950008 (reactive). The guard improved on stalled full by 0.039009 Mbps but remained 0.003514 Mbps below reactive. Its mean trial p95 RTT was still 51.629 ms versus reactive 0.743 ms because many requests completed before the delayed transition. This is **development evidence on a previously inspected source**, not a confirmatory finding; see `results/liveness-development-20261011/`.

A separate protocol-frozen check on a different normal file from the [Zero-SWARM record](https://doi.org/10.5281/zenodo.15082260) completed 15 captured trials. Means were 0.952592 Mbps for original full, 0.952018 for guard and 0.952738 for reactive; all three policies physically committed once in every seed. The escape condition never activated. These tiny mixed differences test that the guard did not cause a large obvious regression on this distinct file; they **do not** test whether it solves high-uncertainty stalls on unseen workloads. The earlier Zero-SWARM normal-file full/reactive comparison was slightly positive for full, while the three PLC-port members were negative. These mixed source-dependent findings preclude a general superiority claim. See `results/zeroswarm-transfer-20261010/` and `results/zeroswarm-large-guard-20261011/`.

### 5.3. Signal and state-integrity boundaries

The lab now records socket-accepted payload rate separately from scheduled input and completed-request goodput. In a frozen ten-trial static-path diagnostic across five synthetic demand families, accepted rate and goodput each produced 48 true positives, zero false negatives, six false positives and 66 true negatives against *planned* overload at the same 0.8-Mbps trigger. The accepted signal was slightly closer to scheduled rate (mean absolute difference 0.182442 versus 0.189481 Mbps) but found no additional overload sample. Neither signal measures unconstrained wire-offered demand in the response-gated generator, and this diagnostic did not tune the guard. See `results/accepted-tx-sensor-20261011/`.

In the captured migrations, zero observed sender TCP resets and zero application failures coexist with reverse endpoint packet-instance deficits and repeated payload sequences. These are distinct outcomes: application continuity in a bounded lab does not establish packet-loss freedom or complete TCP sequence/window equivalence. The post-transition RTT diagnostic excludes in-flight successes and does not measure exact outage. Stateful *offline* replay accounts for readiness, cooldown and a single modeled migration after an initial decision; its capacity and action-cost values are proxies, not measured live benefits. No physical emissions or attack prevention were measured.

### 5.4. Second-device synthetic reproducibility

Before second-device access, we committed a fixed host-replication protocol: full and reactive policies, step/bursts/flash generated demand, seeds 191/193/197, three persistent TCP flows, 12 steps and paired captures. The owner reported a separate native CachyOS device, clean at revision `3951e33`, with all 152 unit tests passing. The supplied raw archive contained all 18 completed policy trials. Reanalysis on the development machine verified its source snapshots and paired captures and regenerated all three report sets byte-for-byte. No residual lab namespaces or capture-kernel drops were recorded. Physical device separation is owner-attested rather than independently certified.

| Synthetic family | Full goodput (Mbps) | Reactive goodput (Mbps) | Full − reactive (Mbps) | Full/reactive mean trial p95 RTT (ms) |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Step | 1.087176 | 1.154560 | -0.067384 | 76.591 / 22.459 |
| Bursts | 0.906415 | 0.966261 | -0.059846 | 76.586 / 18.115 |
| Flash | 0.765021 | 0.830272 | -0.065251 | 90.980 / 2.828 |

Both policies committed once in every trial and all application requests completed. Nonzero endpoint packet-instance deficits and repeated payload-sequence instances occurred in every trial despite clean captures. The unfavorable full-policy goodput differences thus also appear in a setting without the PLC-port no-commit stall. The mechanism behind the differences is not isolated, and the three synthetic families are not independent real workloads. This is cross-device lab reproducibility, **not** a migration between physical machines or a confirmatory superiority test. Complete provenance and descriptive paired intervals are in `results/second-host-20261013/README.md`.

## 6. Validity, ethics and reproducibility

Short isolated-host experiments cannot represent production failure domains or multi-host timing. The second device repeats the lab on a different reported host, but client, firewalls and server still run inside namespaces on that one device per trial. The synchronous client can censor offered demand under congestion. RTT includes only completed requests; failures must be reported alongside it. Local queue drops, request timeouts and end-to-end packet loss are different metrics. In-flight updates, complete TCP sequence/window consistency, packet-level disruption, physical cross-host migration and unseen representative-workload confirmation remain open.

All traffic and faults stay inside the isolated local lab. No third-party attack traffic is generated. Raw public-trace captures are excluded from the repository. New batches archive executed source/config files; older pilots contain fingerprints but no recoverable exact source snapshot. Artifact instructions and the claim ledger distinguish measured, modeled and unverified quantities.

## 7. Conclusion and required final work

The prototype makes policy decisions and their safety checks inspectable on a real software-firewall path. The clearest observed contribution is a reproducible condition under which a fixed forecast-uncertainty gate stalls warm migration and harms service relative to a reactive baseline. A separate synthetic second-device check reproduces the lab pipeline and an unfavorable full-versus-reactive comparison even when both policies migrate. The experimental escape restores a transition in one development setting but has not been confirmed in an unseen high-uncertainty workload. A final submission requires an independently selected confirmatory real-workload setting, measured or explicitly bounded carbon/risk inputs, stronger state/recovery evidence, complete direct-comparator review, resolved artifact terms and owner-selected venue/authors. The publication claim ledger is the acceptance checklist; no generic performance, safety or novelty claim should be inferred from this draft.
